Build caps and the base-year build lock

Without a limit on yearly building, the least-cost solve of OSTRAM could add any amount of the cheapest technology in a single year. A build cap is the most new capacity a technology may add in one year. Between the minimum investment and the cap, cost decides.

Three limits and three special values

Minimum investmentTotalAnnualMinCapacityInvestmentNew capacity that must be built in one year

In the final model a cap of 0 allows nothing, 9999 is a cap so large that it never binds, and -1 or a missing row means no constraint at all. Older training material said that a cap of 0 means no limit; that is not how the model reads it.

How a cap is set

The build-cap rule writes the cap of every plant that has existing capacity or a minimum investment. It starts from how much the existing fleet would have to grow to keep up with demand, then applies, in order, a multiplier per scenario, limits per technology family, a top-up for technologies left with too little room, and a top-up for nodes whose caps add up to too little of the demand-scaled fleet. Each step reads the result of the step before. The settings of each root are in its build_cap_rule.yaml; the guide below lists the released values.

Investment surges

Sometimes a build year spikes: far more capacity appears in one year than in the years around it. In OSTRAM a spike can come from the inputs, as the steps below show.

  1. 1

    A minimum investment sits in one year column

    A plant list that gives a whole programme one commissioning date, or a placeholder date for projects whose date is unknown, puts all of it into one year of the workbook.

  2. 2

    The cap follows the minimum

    A cap below its minimum would make the model infeasible, so the build-cap rule raises the cap just above the minimum of the same year.

  3. 3

    The solver must build it

    The minimum is a rule, so the capacity appears in that year whatever it costs.

  4. 4

    Every scenario inherits the spike

    The roots take their forced builds from the same rows, so the spike shows in all of them. That is the sign that it comes from the data.

What smooths it. The fix is in the data: spread the minimums back over the years of construction, keeping their total, so the capacity still exists by its deadline. The model's own structure smooths the rest:

  • Retirement. The retirement rule draws existing thermal plants down year by year over their lives instead of all at once, so replacement needs arrive gradually.
  • Build caps. Each year can add only so much, so a need is spread over several years.
  • The top-ups. Late in the horizon the retired fleet is small and the formula would give little room; the top-ups keep enough room open that demand can still be met without the backstop.
  • The lock and the delays. The base years build only what is listed, storage waits for its delay to end, and cross-border links grow at a set rate after their lead time. Each of these moves building out of the early years, so a first free year can show catch-up building; check it in a solved run.

Smooth demand growth and steady retirement do not create spikes on their own; when a spike appears, look first for forced builds in the year it appears.

The base-year build lock in brief

The first years of the horizon are history or already under construction. In those years no plant, storage unit or interconnector may add more than the workbook lists for it; the backstop stays open. The lock years and the checks that run with them are in the guide below.
