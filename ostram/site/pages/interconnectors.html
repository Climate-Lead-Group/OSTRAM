Interconnector costs and direction

An interconnector is a technology that takes power from the demand fuel of one node and delivers it, minus its own loss, to the import fuel of another. Like any technology it has a capital cost, a fixed cost, a life and a capacity that the model may expand. South Asia has {{v:south_asia.interconnectors}} of them.

How a link works

  • Two modes. Mode 1 carries power from the first node in the name to the second, mode 2 the other way. One technology serves both directions and shares one capacity.
  • Loss, not cost. The share of power lost on the way is set by the output ratio of the link, from distance and line type. It is not part of the cost.
  • Direction. A scenario can make a link one-way by zeroing one of its modes; see direction below.

Worked example: reading a corridor cost

Take the first row of the South Asia table. Its capital cost is the sum of the parts of the project (converter, line, substation), each brought to the cost year with the World Bank manufactures unit value index, divided by the usable transfer capacity. Its fixed cost is a share of the capital cost paid every year. The low and high values bound the cost sensitivity scenarios. The guide below works the arithmetic through for one corridor.

Direction

Some scenarios study a link that may only run one way, for example when a contract fixes the direction of trade. The direction rule zeroes one mode of each listed link; the scenario files that use it are listed on the scenarios page, and the sources for each direction are in the reference below.
